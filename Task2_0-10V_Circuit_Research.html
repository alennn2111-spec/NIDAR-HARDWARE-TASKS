Summary. The proposed baseline is: Blue Pill → I²C → MCP4725 DAC on 3.3 V → LM358 non-inverting amplifier with gain ≈ 3.03 on a +15 V rail → 47 Ω series resistor and TVS clamp → 0–10 V output. It uses few parts, all available in breadboard-friendly form. Its main limits are that the DAC’s accuracy is much worse than its 12-bit resolution suggests, the 0 V and 10 V ends are the weakest points, and the DAC’s power-up default value must be changed before connecting real equipment.

1How an MCU generates an adjustable analog signal

A microcontroller works with integers. To make a voltage it hands an integer code to a digital-to-analog converter (DAC). For an N-bit DAC with reference voltage VREF, the ideal output is:

V_dac = V_REF × code / 2^N

The signal is adjustable because firmware can write a new code at any time, and the DAC holds its last value until the next write. The MCP4725 used here is a 12-bit part whose reference is its own supply pin, so its output spans 0 V to VDD[1].

Two things stop the DAC from driving a 0–10 V line directly. First, powered from 3.3 V it can only reach about 3.3 V. Second, its output stage is specified for light loads, with a datasheet figure of 5 kΩ and up to 1000 pF[1]. An op-amp fixes both. It multiplies the DAC voltage by a fixed gain and drives the load, and it can run from a higher supply rail than the DAC.

An external DAC is needed because the STM32F103C8 has no on-chip DAC; its datasheet lists ADCs, timers and serial interfaces, and its supply and I/O range is 2.0–3.6 V[7].

V_out = G × V_DD × code / 4096 G = 1 + R_f / R_g

2A beginner-friendly circuit for the STM32 Blue Pill

The Blue Pill carries the STM32F103C8T6, a 72 MHz Arm Cortex-M3 with two I²C and two SPI interfaces[7]. The Blue Pill is a third-party board, so its pinout and on-board 3.3 V regulator vary between makers and clones. Check your own board before wiring.

Proposed baseline Proposed: MCP4725 DAC on I²C, LM358 op-amp in a non-inverting gain stage, a trimmer for full-scale calibration, and a small protected output stage. The reasons it suits a first build:

  • Two signal wires. I²C needs only SCL and SDA. The STM32 I2C1 peripheral is normally on PB6 (SCL) and PB7 (SDA)[7].
  • Matching logic levels. The MCP4725 reads an input as high above 0.7 × VDD[1]. At 3.3 V that is 2.31 V, which a 3.3 V STM32 meets. At a 5 V supply it would be 3.5 V, which a 3.3 V bus cannot guarantee Calculated. So the DAC is powered from 3.3 V and needs no level shifter.
  • Few, common parts. One DAC, one op-amp, two resistors plus a trimmer, and decoupling. The LM358 is available in a DIP-8 package that plugs into a breadboard, and MCP4725 breakout boards are widely sold.
  • Upgrade path. If more resolution, a second channel or an external reference is needed later, the SPI parts in section 4 can replace the DAC with the same op-amp stage.

A PWM output filtered by an RC network is another way to make an analog level. It is not evaluated here because the project has chosen the external-DAC route.

3Stage-by-stage explanation

Figure 2 in section 5 shows these stages as a schematic.

3.1 DAC stage (MCP4725)

  • Single supply of 2.7–5.5 V, 12-bit buffered output, I²C up to 3.4 Mb/s, 6 µs typical settling[1] Datasheet.
  • VDD is also the reference, so noise or error on the 3.3 V rail passes straight to the output. The datasheet suggests a 0.1 µF ceramic plus a 10 µF capacitor close to the pin[1] Datasheet.
  • SDA and SCL are open-drain and need pull-ups; 1–10 kΩ is the typical range given[1]. Many breakout boards already include them.
  • The “fast mode” write updates the output without touching the on-chip EEPROM, which is rated for 1 million write cycles[1]. Use fast mode for normal operation.
  • For gain error under 1 % of full scale, the datasheet advises using codes 100 to 4000 rather than the full range[1] Datasheet. The 7-bit address is 0x60 or 0x61 for the A0 variant and 0x62 or 0x63 for the A1 variant, set by the A0 pin[1].

3.2 Op-amp and feedback resistors

The op-amp is a non-inverting amplifier. The DAC drives the + input, and a divider from the output to the − input sets the gain:

G = 1 + R_f / R_g

The target gain is 10 V / VDD. With VDD = 3.3 V, G = 3.03, so Rf/Rg = 2.03 Calculated. The 3.3 V rail will not be exact, so a fixed resistor pair would miss 10 V. The proposal is Rg = 10 kΩ and Rf = 18 kΩ in series with a 5 kΩ multi-turn trimmer, which adjusts Rf/Rg from 1.8 to 2.3 Proposed.

The divider (Rg + Rf ≈ 30 kΩ) draws only about 0.33 mA at 10 V Calculated, so it adds little load. Use 1 % metal-film resistors and keep the trimmer’s temperature drift in mind.

For a single-supply LM358 the output can swing down to about ground, with a typical headroom to the negative rail of 5 mV (no load)[5] Datasheet.

3.3 Power supply

  • 3.3 V rail. From the Blue Pill’s on-board regulator, which is fed by USB 5 V. It powers the MCU and the DAC. Because it is the DAC reference, a separate low-noise 3.3 V regulator or a filtered tap is a worthwhile later improvement Proposed.
  • Op-amp rail: +15 V. The LM358’s output can only rise to about 1.5–2 V below its supply[5][8] Datasheet. On 12 V that leaves roughly 10–10.5 V, no margin for a loaded 10 V output. On 15 V the ceiling is about 13 V Calculated. The LM358-N is specified up to 32 V[5], so 15 V is comfortable.
  • Rail-to-rail alternative. A rail-to-rail output op-amp such as the OPA192 (4.5–36 V) reaches within about 0.1 V of the rail at a 10 kΩ load[6], so it could run from a 12 V rail.
  • Decoupling and grounding. 100 nF plus 10 µF at the op-amp supply pins, and one common ground shared by the Blue Pill, DAC, op-amp and output ground. Keep the DAC’s ground return on a clean path.

3.4 Output stage

  • Series resistor R3 (47 Ω). Isolates the op-amp from cable capacitance and limits fault current. The feedback is taken before R3, so a load RL sees a small drop of R3 / (R3 + RL), about 0.47 % into 10 kΩ Calculated.
  • TVS diode D1. A unidirectional clamp from the output to ground for transients and miswiring. Its voltage rating must sit above 10 V plus margin. The exact part is to be chosen Proposed.
  • Optional C3 (10 nF). Adds high-frequency noise filtering. With a 47 Ω R3 the corner is near 340 kHz Calculated.
  • Not isolated. The output shares ground with the Blue Pill. Receiving equipment is normally a high-impedance input (10 kΩ or more), which keeps load current near or below 1 mA at 10 V.

Illustrative firmware sketch

This shows how the DAC code is sent and how a voltage maps to a code. It is a reading aid for the datasheet’s fast-mode write format[1]. Not done It has not been compiled, run or tested on hardware.

/* Illustrative only. Not compiled or tested. */
#define DAC_ADDR      (0x60 << 1)  /* 7-bit 0x60: A0-variant part, A0 pin low. Check yours. */
#define FULL_SCALE_V  10.0f        /* G x VDD, to be set by calibration */

static void dac_write(uint16_t code)       /* code 0..4095 */
{
    uint8_t buf[2];
    buf[0] = (uint8_t)((code >> 8) & 0x0F); /* fast mode: C2=C1=0, PD1=PD0=0, D11..D8 */
    buf[1] = (uint8_t)(code & 0xFF);        /* D7..D0 */
    HAL_I2C_Master_Transmit(&hi2c1, DAC_ADDR, buf, 2, 10);
}

void set_output_volts(float v)
{
    float code = v / FULL_SCALE_V * 4096.0f;
    if (code < 0.0f)    code = 0.0f;
    if (code > 4095.0f) code = 4095.0f;     /* datasheet suggests 100..4000 for best linearity */
    dac_write((uint16_t)(code + 0.5f));
}

6Advantages and limitations of the proposed circuit

Advantages

  • Simple. One DAC, one op-amp, a few passives; two signal wires from the MCU.
  • Breadboard-friendly. The LM358 comes in DIP-8, and MCP4725 breakouts are common.
  • No level shifting. The DAC runs at the MCU’s 3.3 V logic level[1][7].
  • Protected DAC. The op-amp buffers the DAC from the load, which the DAC alone could only drive down to about 5 kΩ[1].
  • Calibratable. The trimmer sets full scale, and software can correct offset.
  • Clear upgrade paths. OPA192, MCP4922, DAC8552 or DAC8760 can each address a specific weakness (section 4).

Limitations

  • Accuracy is far below 12 bits. See the error budget below.
  • Reference is the 3.3 V rail. Its error and noise pass to the output, multiplied by the gain[1].
  • Ends of the range are weakest. The datasheet advises codes 100–4000 for best gain accuracy[1], and a single-supply LM358 cannot reach exactly 0 V.
  • Power-up value is mid-scale by default. The factory EEPROM setting gives half of VDD at start-up[1], which here is about 5 V at the output. The EEPROM must be programmed to code 0 before connecting real equipment, and normal writes should use fast mode.
  • Needs an extra +15 V rail with the LM358.
  • Voltage source only. It cannot sink current, so it does not suit 0–10 V lighting interfaces where the controller sinks current.
  • Not isolated or fully field-protected. Behaviour when the +15 V rail is on and the DAC is unpowered is not characterised here; a high-value pull-down on the op-amp + input is a candidate fix to check in simulation.

Planned verification (not yet performed)

  1. Simulate the analog stage. Use the op-amp manufacturer’s SPICE model and an ideal voltage source for the DAC. Check gain, output ceiling on 12 V and 15 V rails, behaviour near 0 V, a load step, and stability with cable capacitance. Also check the unpowered-DAC case from section 6.
  2. Build on a breadboard. Confirm the I²C address, then step through codes 0, 100, 1024, 2048, 3072, 4000 and 4095 while measuring the output with a multimeter. Test 10 kΩ and 2 kΩ loads and record the power-up value.
  3. Calibrate and record. Set full scale with RV1, fit an offset correction, and log error against code. Compare the results with the error budget in section 6.

The team still needs to define pass/fail limits (accuracy, load, power-up value) before these tests. Results should go in a separate report so simulated and measured values stay distinguishable from the research here.
