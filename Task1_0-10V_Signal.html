Summary. A 0–10 V signal is a DC voltage that represents a value, for example a speed or position command, on a straight line from 0 V (minimum) to 10 V (maximum). An STM32 cannot produce 10 V directly. The proposed chain is: the STM32 sends a digital code to an external DAC, and an op-amp stage scales and protects the DAC output to give 0–10 V.

1What a 0–10 V analog signal is

A 0–10 V signal is a widely used industrial control signal. The DC voltage between two conductors, the signal wire and its common (ground) return, carries one value such as a setpoint, a command or a sensor reading. Because the voltage can take any value in the range, the signal is called analog.

The mapping from value to voltage is linear. The bottom of the range is 0 V and the top is 10 V:

V = 10 V × (value − min) / (max − min)

As an example, a motor drive set up to run from 0 Hz to 50 Hz over its analog input would run at 25 Hz when it receives 5 V. The scaling lives in the receiving device, so the output side only needs to deliver the correct voltage.

It is a voltage-mode signal. The sender holds a voltage and the receiver reads it with a high-impedance input, so almost no current flows. This is what separates it from the 4–20 mA current loop, which is covered in the next section.

2Voltage range and signal behavior

The nominal range is 0 V to 10 V DC. The output follows the commanded value in proportion, so the graph is a straight line from the origin.

  • Proportional. 2.5 V is 25 %, 5 V is 50 %, 10 V is 100 %.
  • Single-ended. The voltage is measured against a common return, so sender and receiver must share a reference.
  • Continuous in theory, stepped in practice. A DAC produces discrete steps, and the step size depends on its resolution (see section 4).

Practical limits to design for

  • Load current. Receiving inputs are commonly in the range of several kΩ to 100 kΩ or more, but this varies by product. At 10 V, a 10 kΩ input draws 1 mA, so the output stage must be able to source the load current of the lowest-impedance input it will serve.
  • Noise and cable length. Voltage signals pick up interference and suffer ground offsets, so they suit short runs and are normally wired with shielded twisted pair.
  • No live zero. A reading of 0 V can mean a valid minimum or a broken wire, a failed source or lost power. The receiver cannot tell which.
  • Overrange. Some receivers accept a small margin above 10 V and others do not. Treat 10 V as a hard ceiling unless the receiving device states otherwise.

3Common applications in industrial control

The signal is common where a controller sends a command to a nearby device, or a transmitter reports a value to a local controller.

  • Variable-frequency drives and motor controllers. A 0–10 V input is a standard way to set speed. Many EC fans accept it as well.
  • HVAC. Damper and valve actuators, and fan or pump speed control, are often positioned by a 0–10 V command.
  • Lighting control. 0–10 V dimming is a common interface for LED drivers and ballasts. These typically work by sinking a small current rather than receiving a driven voltage, so the interface needs checking against the driver datasheet.
  • PLC and DCS analog I/O. Analog input and output modules commonly offer 0–10 V as a selectable range.
  • Process transmitters. Pressure, temperature, level and flow sensors often have a 0–10 V output option.
  • Power controllers. Heater and SCR power controllers can take a 0–10 V reference to set output power.

In each case the receiver is expected to treat 0 V as the lowest value and 10 V as the highest. Our hardware therefore has to be accurate at both ends of the range.

4How STM32 + an external DAC can generate the signal

An STM32 works from a 3.3 V supply, so its pins and its internal DAC (on the variants that have one) cannot go above that level. The internal DAC is also limited in drive capability. Generating 0–10 V needs a DAC and an amplifier stage, and an external DAC is attractive because it offers higher resolution options, a dedicated reference voltage and multiple channels.

Signal chain, step by step

  1. Firmware computes a code. For an N-bit DAC, the code for a target voltage is below.
code = round( V_target / 10 V × 2^N ), limited to 2^N − 1
  1. STM32 sends the code to the DAC over SPI or I²C. Check that the DAC's logic input levels accept 3.3 V signals.
  2. DAC outputs 0 to VREF. The ideal output is VREF × code / 2N. The reference voltage sets accuracy and stability.
  3. Signal conditioning scales it to 0–10 V. A non-inverting op-amp stage supplies the gain.
G = 10 V / V_REF = 1 + R_f / R_g

Resolution

With the gain set so that full scale is 10 V, one step (LSB) at the output is 10 V / 2N. The top code gives one LSB below 10 V, because a DAC reaches VREF only in the limit. Whether to trim the gain slightly to reach exactly 10 V is a decision for the design stage.

Step size is only the resolution. Absolute accuracy also depends on reference error, op-amp offset, resistor tolerance and DAC linearity. These need to be taken from datasheets and, later, from measurement.

Signal conditioning design points

  • Supply headroom. The op-amp must be powered from a rail comfortably above 10 V, for example 12 V, 15 V or a 24 V field supply, so that its output can reach 10 V under load.
  • Reaching 0 V. A single-supply op-amp output cannot reach exactly 0 V. Options are a rail-to-rail output part that swings within millivolts of ground, or a small negative supply. This determines how accurately the bottom of the range can be reproduced.
  • Drive and stability. The stage must source the expected load current and stay stable with cable capacitance, usually by adding a small series output resistor.
  • Protection. Clamp or TVS devices and an RC filter protect the output against ESD, miswiring and noise. Isolation is optional and depends on the system grounding.

Candidate parts to evaluate

None of these has been selected. DAC examples include the MCP4725 (12-bit, I²C), MCP4922 (dual 12-bit, SPI) and DAC8552 (dual 16-bit, SPI). Integrated industrial output DACs, such as the TI DAC8760 family, offer voltage-output ranges with the output stage built in. This could replace the discrete op-amp stage at a higher part cost. Verify every part against its current datasheet before use.

Objective

The objective of the NIDAR 2.0 hardware effort for this signal is to generate a stable, controllable 0–10 V analog output from an STM32 using an external DAC and a signal-conditioning stage. Task 1 establishes the shared understanding and the proposed signal chain.

Proposed next steps

  • Confirm the receiving device and its input impedance and accuracy needs.
  • Choose the DAC resolution, reference and interface (SPI or I²C).
  • Design and simulate the op-amp stage, including 0 V behavior and load drive.
  • Write STM32 firmware that maps a requested value to a DAC code.
  • Build a prototype and verify the output range and accuracy by measurement. This step has not been done.

6Block diagram

DAC

0–10 V Output

The signal delivered to the external device, wired with a signal common return.
